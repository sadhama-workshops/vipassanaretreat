Search for "meditation" and you'll see someone sitting on a mountain top in full lotus, perfectly serene. For the first couple of days of a retreat, that won't be you. And that's fine.

So let me tell you honestly what a vipassana retreat is, as someone who sits one every year and teaches them at Hardwick, on the banks of the Thames.

## Vipassana is the result, not the technique

*Vipassanā* is a Pāḷi word. *Passanā* means seeing, and *vi* means clearly, in a special way. So vipassana means seeing clearly: seeing things as they really are.

People talk about "doing vipassana" as if it were a method. Strictly, vipassana is what you get. The method is mindfulness, *sati*, practised according to the Buddha's teaching on the four foundations of mindfulness, the Satipaṭṭhāna. Every well-known vipassana tradition goes back to this teaching. You can read the Buddha's own words on it in the [Satipaṭṭhāna Saṁyutta (SN 47)](/library/read.html#samyutta-nikaya-sn47) in our library.

## A retreat for training the mind

This is not a chanting retreat or a visualisation retreat, and nobody will try to convert you to anything. It is quiet, and we work with the raw material of experience: seeing, hearing, touching, thinking.

The Pāḷi word for meditation, *bhāvanā*, means cultivation. Just as the gym trains the body, here we train the mind: awareness, energy, concentration, confidence and wisdom.

There are really two kinds of retreat. In one, the body comes to the retreat but the mind stays at home, planning and remembering. In the other, the mind comes too. That second kind is what the whole day is designed for.

## What you actually do all day

We teach in the tradition of **Mahasi Sayadaw**, the noting method practised by monks and nuns in Burma and Thailand.

- **Sitting meditation.** You watch the rising and falling of the belly and note it softly in the mind: *rising, falling*. When the mind wanders, you note that too, *thinking, thinking*, and come back.
- **Walking meditation.** Slow, precise steps: *lifting, moving, placing*. It is far more than stretching your legs. Many people have their first clear moments of insight while walking.
- **Mindfulness in daily activities.** Eating, washing, walking back to your tent. Every movement becomes practice.
- **Dhamma talks** that explain the practice and the Buddha's teaching in plain language.
- **Personal interviews** with the teacher, so your practice is checked and guided.
- **Gentle yoga** each morning, to help the body sit comfortably.

The heart of it fits in three words: **note, know, let go.** Be aware of what is happening. Know it wisely, as just a passing thing, not "me" or "mine". Then let it go.

## Why silence?

Silence is not a punishment. It's a gift. You don't have to be anybody for anyone: no small talk, no performing, no phone. All the energy that usually pours out through conversation stays with the practice.

Most people are surprised by how nourishing it is. By the third day, many say it is the most restful thing they have done in years.

## A typical day

The schedule follows the rhythm of a monastery. A bell tells you where to be, so you never need to check the time.

- 4:30 wake up
- 5:00 sitting meditation, then meditative yoga, then sitting again
- Breakfast and rest
- Instructions, then walking and sitting through the morning
- Lunch and rest
- Walking and sitting through the afternoon, with teacher interviews
- Evening Dhamma talk or loving-kindness (*mettā*) meditation
- 21:30 sleep

## Is it hard?

For a couple of days, yes. The body complains and the mind negotiates: "What about that knee? Perhaps you should go and lie down." It always finds a new reason.

This is normal, like soreness after the gym, and it leads to strength. Usually by the third day the body adjusts, the mind settles, and the real work begins. Towards the end, many people have more energy than they've had in years.

It helps to be well looked after. At Hardwick your meals are cooked for you, there is a wood-fired sauna by the river for warming tired bones, and therapists are on hand for massage if sitting has left you stiff.

## What do you take home?

Nothing exotic. A skill. When anger, worry or sadness comes, you can step back and see it as something that arises, stays a while, and passes. While you take it personally, it keeps pushing your buttons. Once you see it isn't you, it can let go.

The Buddha said that just as the ocean has one taste, the taste of salt, his teaching has one taste, the taste of freedom ([Ud 5.5](/library/read.html#udana-vagga5)). That is what we are tasting.

## Try it in the right place

A vipassana retreat works best where everything else is taken care of: simple, nourishing food on time, a quiet place, a teacher who knows the path, and people around you doing the same work. Alone, waking at 4:30 is almost impossible. Together, it is surprisingly easy.
