*"A big distraction for me is the back pain from sitting in this posture. Is there anything you can do about that?"*

I hear this on every retreat. Pain and the wandering mind are the two big obstacles at the start. The good news: pain you can work with. And it becomes one of your best teachers.

First, the practical side. Then, the meditation side.

## 1. Look after the body

You don't need to suffer for the sake of suffering.

- **Raise your hips.** Sit on the front edge of a firm cushion, so the knees are lower than the hips. This keeps the spine upright without effort.
- **Try different seats.** Cross-legged, kneeling on a bench, or on a chair with feet flat on the ground. All are fine.
- **Stack your spine.** Imagine the cobra: stretched out on the ground, the eagle can catch it. Coiled and upright, nobody can touch it. Upright, but not rigid.
- **Move between sits.** At our retreats we alternate 45 minutes of sitting with 45 minutes of walking, and do yoga every morning. Yoga asana was designed for exactly this, to prepare the body for meditation.
- **If it's an injury or chronic pain,** change position, use a chair, and tell the teacher. That's wisdom, not weakness.

Even my legs go numb sometimes. Teachers too.

## 2. Pain is a concept

Now the interesting part.

"Pain" is a label. A concept. When you look closely, what's actually there? Burning. Pressure. Twisting. Throbbing. Heat. These are the raw data, the real sensations.

In the noting practice, we note the process, not the concept. So instead of "my knee pain, my knee pain", you go into the centre of the sensation and note what's really there: *pressure, pressure. Burning, burning. Heat.*

Look how it moves. It gets stronger, weaker, shifts a little to the left, pulses. It's not one solid block of pain. It's a stream of changing sensations.

## 3. Add the feeling

There's also the feeling tone: unpleasant. That's another foundation of mindfulness, *vedanā*. Note it: *unpleasant, unpleasant.*

And there's the mind's reaction: *disliking, wanting it to stop, fear it will get worse.* Note that too.

Often, most of the suffering is in the reaction, not in the knee. The mind as a character: *"Oh, this knee. It's going to be damaged. They'll probably have to amputate it."* Don't worry. Nobody has ever had their legs cut off at a meditation retreat.

## 4. Two strategies

**Stay with it.** For non-chronic pain, you can make it your main object for a while. Go right into it, note it precisely, moment by moment. Every retreat, someone comes to me and says, "Whoa, I did this, and poof, it was gone."

**Keep it secondary.** Or, keep rising and falling as your main object, and include the sensation: *rising, falling, heat. Rising, falling, pressure.* When it's no longer pulling you, let it go.

If after a long, honest try the pain is still too strong, move mindfully. Note *intending to move, lifting, moving, placing*. Then continue.

## Why this matters off the cushion

When you learn to see pain as changing sensations that aren't "mine", something big shifts. The same skill works with emotional pain: anxiety, sadness, anger. Until you take it personally, it hangs around. Once you see it as a thing, the characteristics of the thing kick in. It arises. It passes.

So pain isn't a disturbance to your meditation. Nothing is. The best time to practise is when you suffer the most, because then there's something real to practise on.
