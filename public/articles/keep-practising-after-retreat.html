After the Retreat: How to Keep Your Meditation Practice Going at Home

By Dmitry Glazkov, vipassana teacher · 3 min read

On the last morning of every retreat, we talk about going home. You've done it. Feels good, doesn't it? Not pushed into the corner by wants and don't-wants. Just the middle.

Now, how do you keep it?

It works like brushing your teeth

You don't clean your teeth very well for seven days and then stop for a month. It doesn't work like that. A little hygiene, every day. Meditation is hygiene for the mind.

So the secret isn't intensity. It's continuity.

Choose something you can keep

Right after a retreat, motivation is high. People decide on two and a half hours every morning. For two weeks, maybe. Then you oversleep once, you travel, and it's gone.

Better: choose something modest you can actually maintain, and add later.

  • Beginners: 15 to 20 minutes, once a day.
  • More experienced: 45 minutes to an hour keeps the mind clear.

Make it non-negotiable. You don't decide every day, "Do I do it today? What time?" It's just there, like the teeth.

Two good times

Early morning. It's 5am. Do you have time? Everyone has time at 5am. Some at 6. Put it where you have time.

Before sleep. The Buddha described the drawbacks of falling asleep "unmindful and unaware": you sleep badly and wake miserably, and you have bad dreams. Fall asleep mindful, and you sleep at ease and wake happily (AN 5.210). Tidy up the mind at the end of the day. Rising, falling, until you're gone.

Three minutes every hour

Here's a little trick. Throughout the day, come back for three minutes. Link it to something you already do: before your cup of tea, after lunch, when you sit in the car before driving. In the Thich Nhat Hanh monasteries a bell rings at random, and everyone stops and comes back. You can set your own bell.

And when you wait, meditate. In a queue? Rising, falling. On the platform? Lifting, placing, very slowly. Waiting time is free practice time.

Make a place

A corner at home, a cushion, maybe a candle. When you see it, it invites you.

Keep the mind afloat with the teachings

Read a little of the original teachings regularly. The Buddha's own words. A few verses of the Dhammapada with your morning tea. A short sutta from the Aṅguttara Nikāya before bed. Or press "Listen" in our library and let it read to you.

The worldly winds will blow

When you go home, life is waiting. The Buddha called them the eight worldly conditions: gain and loss, fame and obscurity, praise and blame, pleasure and pain. You can't escape them while living the lay life.

What changes is how you meet them. You start taking things a little less personally. Praise and blame, like water off a goose. And as you take things less personally, the suffering gets less. That's the whole point.

Keep the three pillars

For lay people, the Buddha gave three: virtuous behaviour (the five precepts), generosity, and meditation. Keep all three a little, and they support each other.

Come back

Most people get their real breakthrough on their second or third retreat. Every year I sit a month-long or 21-day retreat myself. A retreat once or twice a year recharges what you build every day at home.
