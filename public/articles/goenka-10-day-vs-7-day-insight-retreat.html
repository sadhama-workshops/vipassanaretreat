Goenka 10-Day Vipassana vs a 7-Day Insight Retreat: Which Is Right for You?

By Dmitry Glazkov, vipassana teacher · 3 min read

Most people who look up "vipassana" have heard of the ten-day courses in the tradition of S.N. Goenka. They are wonderful. They have brought this practice to millions of people, freely, all over the world, and many of the people who sit with us at Hardwick did one first.

In the UK those courses run at Dhamma Dīpa in Herefordshire and Dhamma Sukhakārī in Suffolk, and places often go to a waiting list. So when people find our seven-day Insight Vipassana retreat, they usually ask three questions. How is it different? Is seven days really enough? And if I've already learned the Goenka technique, can I combine the two?

Here are my honest answers.

Same root, different method

Both traditions come from the same teaching: the Buddha's four foundations of mindfulness, the Satipaṭṭhāna. Every internationally known vipassana tradition rests on it. What differs is how the training is done.

Goenka tradition (from Sayagyi U Ba Khin, Burma): three days of ānāpāna, attention on the breath at the nostrils, then systematic body scanning, observing sensations with equanimity. Instruction is mainly by recorded audio and video.

Mahasi tradition (from Mahasi Sayadaw, Burma), which we teach: attention on the rising and falling of the belly, with gentle mental noting, rising, falling, and noting whatever pulls the mind away: thinking, hearing, pain, liking. Walking meditation with the same care: lifting, moving, placing. This is the method monks and nuns study in Burma and Thailand today.

Neither is better. They are different paths up the same mountain.

Comparison at a glance

Goenka 10-day course Our 7-day Insight Vipassana
Length 10 full days plus arrival and departure 7 days, with 5 full days of silence
Technique Breath at the nostrils, then body scanning Rising and falling, mental noting, all four foundations
Walking meditation Not part of the formal practice Formal practice, alternating with sitting
Teacher Recorded talks; assistant teachers answer questions Live Dhamma talks and personal interviews with the teacher
Movement No yoga or exercise Gentle yoga every morning to prepare the body
Sitting Group sits of an hour or more 45-minute sits alternating with 45 minutes of walking
Setting Purpose-built centre, mostly indoors Marquee in a meadow by the Thames, with tents and a riverside sauna
Food Simple vegetarian meals Ayurvedic plant-based cooking by Karen, much of it from the estate's walled garden
Cost Entirely donation-based A small fee for food and camping; the teaching is offered for donation

If you already practise another technique

Goenka himself uses a lovely image for this: trying to ride two horses at once. A circus rider might manage it. The rest of us fall off.

The concentration you built with body scanning will serve you well. But during the retreat, put the old technique to one side and give the noting method your full effort. Any meditation works only when it has your whole heart. Switching between methods mid-retreat brings confusion very quickly.

At home afterwards, practise whichever one truly works for you.

Is seven days enough?

In the Satipaṭṭhāna the Buddha says that someone who develops the four foundations properly may see results within seven years, or seven months, or even seven days. That is not a promise of enlightenment by Sunday. But a week of continuous practice is enough to learn the method well and to taste real concentration and insight. Many people have their first clear experience of the present moment on a seven-day retreat.

It is also a kind first step if ten days feels like too much, or you can't take that long away from work and family.

Which might suit you?

A Goenka course may suit you if you want a fully donation-based course, a longer container, a purpose-built indoor centre, and a standardised format you can repeat anywhere in the world.

An insight retreat like ours may suit you if you would like walking meditation, daily yoga, a teacher you can sit down and talk with, a method you can carry into every moment of the day, and the softness of the countryside around you: dawn mist on the river, the old oaks, a warm sauna after a long day of sitting, and a massage therapist on hand for tired shoulders.

Many people do both over the years, and that is fine. The Dhamma isn't a brand.
