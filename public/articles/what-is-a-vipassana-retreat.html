What Is a Vipassana Retreat? An Honest Guide from a Teacher

By Dmitry Glazkov, vipassana teacher · 4 min read

When you type "meditation" into Google, what do you see? Someone sitting on top of a mountain, in full lotus, everything serene. Yes, that's your expectation. For a couple of days, it won't be you. And that's fine.

So let me tell you honestly what a vipassana retreat is, from the point of view of someone who sits them every year and teaches them.

Vipassana is the result, not the technique

Vipassanā is a Pāḷi word. Passanā is seeing, and vi is clearly, in a special way. So vipassana means seeing clearly, seeing things as they really are.

People talk about "doing vipassana" as if it were a method. Strictly, vipassana is what you get. The method is mindfulness, sati, practised according to the Buddha's teaching on the four foundations of mindfulness, the Satipaṭṭhāna. Every well-known vipassana tradition in the world goes back to this teaching. You can read the Buddha's own discourses on it in the Satipaṭṭhāna Saṁyutta (SN 47) in our library.

It's a mind-training retreat

This is not a chanting retreat. Not a visualisation retreat. Nobody is trying to convert you to anything. It's quiet, and we deal with the raw data of experience: seeing, hearing, touching, thinking.

Meditation, in Pāḷi, is bhāvanā. It means cultivation, development of the mind. Just as the gym trains the body, here we train the mind: awareness, energy, concentration, confidence, wisdom.

The Buddha said there are two kinds of retreat. In one, you bring your body to the retreat, but the mind is still at home, planning, remembering, tightening the knots. A friend once told me about her ten-day retreat: "Wonderful. So much time for thinking." Well. In the other kind, the mind comes to the retreat too. That's the one we want.

What you actually do all day

At our retreats we teach in the tradition of Mahasi Sayadaw, the noting method practised by monks and nuns in Burma and Thailand. In short:

  • Sitting meditation. You watch the rising and falling of the belly, and you note it, softly, in the mind: rising, falling. When the mind wanders, you note that too: thinking, thinking. Then back.
  • Walking meditation. Slow, precise steps: lifting, moving, placing. Don't think it's just stretching your legs. Many people have their first clear moments of insight during walking.
  • Noting in daily activities. Eating, washing, walking to the tent. Every move, you are aware.
  • Dhamma talks that explain the practice and the Buddha's teaching in plain language.
  • Personal interviews with the teacher to check your practice.

Our formula is very simple: note, know, let go. Awareness of what's happening. Knowing it with wisdom, as just a thing, not mine, not myself. Then letting it go.

Why silence?

Silence is not a punishment. It's a gift. You don't have to be anybody for anyone. No small talk, no performing, no phone. All the energy that usually goes out through the mouth stays with the practice.

Most people are surprised how nourishing it is. By day three, many say it's the most restful thing they've done in years.

A typical day

The schedule follows monastery life. A bell tells you where to be, so you don't even need to look at a clock.

  • 4:30 wake up
  • 5:00 sitting meditation, then meditative yoga, then sitting again
  • Breakfast, rest
  • Meditation instructions, walking and sitting through the morning
  • Lunch and rest
  • Walking and sitting through the afternoon, interviews with the teacher
  • Evening Dhamma talk, questions, or loving-kindness (mettā) meditation
  • 21:00 to bed

You can see the full timetable on our retreats page.

Is it hard?

For a couple of days, yes. The body complains, the mind negotiates. "Oh, what about your painful knee? You might just go and lie down now." It always finds a nice new reason.

This is normal. It's like soreness after the gym: it leads to adaptation and strength. Usually on day three the body adjusts, the mind settles, and the real work starts. Towards the end, many people have more energy than they've had in years.

What do you take home?

Not something exotic. A skill. When anger comes, or worry, or sadness, you can step out of it and see it as a thing that arises, stays a while, and passes. Until you take it personally, it keeps pushing your buttons. Once you see it's not you, it can let go.

The Buddha said the ocean has one taste, the taste of salt, and in the same way his teaching has one taste, the taste of freedom (Ud 5.5). That's what we're tasting.

Try it, in the right place

A vipassana retreat works best where everything else is taken care of: simple food on time, a quiet place, a teacher who knows the path, and people around you doing the same work. Alone, waking at 4:30 is almost impossible. Together, it's possible.
